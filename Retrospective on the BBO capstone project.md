# Retrospective on the BBO capstone project

## Initial codebase

My starting point came from the course rather than from an external optimisation library or earlier work. The Capstone FAQ sheet set out the workflow: load the initial data, define the search space, fit a surrogate model, choose the next point with an acquisition function, then append each result and repeat. The three Bayesian hyperparameter-optimisation notebooks that the course provided as examples ([Koehrsen](https://github.com/WillKoehrsen/hyperparameter-optimization/blob/master/Bayesian%20Hyperparameter%20Optimization%20of%20Gradient%20Boosting%20Machine.ipynb), [Galli](https://github.com/solegalli/hyperparameter-optimization/blob/master/Section-06-Bayesian-Optimization/06-Bayesian-Optimization-xgb-Optional.ipynb) and [Soham](https://github.com/soham96/hyperparameter_optimisation/blob/master/Bayesian_Optimisation.ipynb)) showed that loop in working code. From the course material I learnt the overall loop, the Upper Confidence Bound and Expected Improvement acquisition functions, and how to plot a model's predictions and uncertainty, and I then applied them to the eight functions.

I used scikit-learn's Gaussian Process because it was one of the course examples and it suits this problem. It works with very few points and returns an uncertainty with every prediction, which the acquisition functions require.

The course material was a starting point, not a finished solution. I adapted it with per-function kernels and exploration settings, a Random Forest as a second model, a duplicate-query check and local search around each best point, which the next section describes week by week.

## Code modification

**The plan.** My mindset from the first query was to explore first and exploit later: spend the early weeks learning where each function was high, then refine around the best points. I also expected to adapt, because exploiting only makes sense once there is something reliable to exploit. In Week 1 I put more emphasis on exploitation for the two-dimensional functions, which I could plot, and more on exploration for the higher-dimensional ones, where there was too little data to know where to refine. I fitted a Gaussian Process for each function, scored 10,000 random candidate points with Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) and Expected Improvement (EI), and set the exploration parameter kappa higher as the dimension increased.

**Weeks 1 to 5: adapting to each result.** When a function improved I lowered kappa to exploit it. When a move failed I changed direction: after exploiting F2 lost ground I raised its exploration, and after exploring near F5's peak gave a lower value I returned to the peak itself. These weeks found the largest early gains (F5's peak of 2,987 in Week 1, and F4 rising from -4.03 to 0.37 by Week 3), but they exposed two problems. F1 stayed flat and F2 could not beat its starting value, and four queries were wasted as exact repeats because UCB and EI kept proposing points I had already sampled.

**Week 6: a second model and a duplicate check.** The repeats showed that the code needed a safeguard, so I blocked any candidate within 0.001 of a sampled point. I also added a Random Forest, out of curiosity about whether a different kind of model could improve on the Gaussian Process. The Gaussian Process gave F5 no signal away from its data, while the Random Forest's prediction landed near F5's best point. All four Random Forest picks fell within about 1.1 standard deviations of their predictions, while the Gaussian Process had missed F4 by about 6. No query has been repeated since.

**Week 7: reading the failures.** The Week 6 results pointed to two problems. F1's outputs ranged from 1e-245 to 1e-3, so models fitted to the raw values could not tell points apart. Modelling the log of the output made the pattern visible, and F1 improved for the first time (7.7e-16 to 1.9e-6). For F3 and F4, the global search kept choosing far-off points that the models themselves predicted to be poor, and F4's wide jump had fallen to -2.83. So I searched a small box around each best point instead, and F4 recovered to 0.27 while F3 set a new best. Meanwhile F7's far-reaching pick scored 0.93 against a predicted 1.70, a miss of 4.1 standard deviations.

**Week 8: the switch to exploitation.** This was the turning point. The evidence was now consistent: wherever I took a wide jump the results were poor, and wherever I stayed near a good point they improved. Most functions also had an established good region, so the second phase of my plan had arrived. I searched a box of ±0.02 around each function's best point and chose the highest predicted value there. All eight functions set a new best for the first time, for example F4 rose from 0.37 to 0.61 and F5 from 2,987 to 3,175. When I prepared the next round I checked whether kappa still mattered inside such a small box. Raising it from 0 to 2 moved most picks by under 0.03, so I treated the box size as my main exploration setting and left kappa alone.

**Weeks 9 to 13: adapting again.** Exploitation then needed the same adaptation that exploration had needed at the start. Where the Gaussian Process's picks sat next to a failed point or predicted nothing above the best, I measured the function directly: line searches and half steps for F4 and F6, a pattern search for F1 (one step of +0.012 in x2 multiplied its output by 12), and in Week 12 a Gaussian Process with a separate length scale for each input on F4. I kept some exploration alive where it was cheap, probing the input boundary for F5 and F7, whose best points sat there, and trying a step at right angles on F4, which gained 0.058 on the final query.

**What mattered most.** The Week 8 switch. Of the 46 new bests across the project, 31 came from Week 8 onwards, and the final query set five more.

## Final result

**How the score evolved in the final weeks.** Once I switched to small steps in Week 8, progress became steady. F5 had sat at 2,987 for seven weeks; it then rose in every one of the last six rounds, to 4,246 (up 42%). F4 went from 0.61 to 0.71, and F1 reached 1.43e-4, roughly eleven orders of magnitude above its starting best, before the peak of its narrow stripe slipped between my last points. F2's best of 0.667 held for five weeks, and then a refinement on the final query raised it to 0.742. F3 and F6 levelled off (F3 at -0.0054 since Week 9, F6 at -0.0575 since Week 12), which fits narrow peaks that one query a week was too slow to locate. Five functions set a new best on the very last query, so F2, F4, F5, F7 and F8 were still improving when the queries ran out.

**What I would do differently.** With more time, I would explore a wider range of models and techniques, and use further libraries such as BoTorch, particularly for the higher-dimensional functions, where 10,000 random candidates searched the space coarsely, and for the functions that levelled off. With a fresh start, I would change five things:
- **Exploit sooner.** F5 found its peak in Week 1, and F4 reached 0.37 by Week 3, yet both stalled for five to seven weeks while I kept exploring. Once I took small steps they improved every week.
- **Build the safeguards on day one.** Four queries were repeats, and I only began logging each prediction's error in Week 6. A duplicate check and a prediction log from Week 1 would have saved queries.
- **Study each function before modelling it.** F1's output range (1e-245 to 1e-3) meant raw-value models could see nothing, and it took until Week 7 to handle that. Checking output scales first would have saved weeks.
- **Record reasons and structure the code from the start.** Some settings have no recorded reason, which I would now avoid by writing each reason down at the time. I would also write the code as a reproducible notebook with relative paths and a requirements file, rather than tidying it at the end.
- **Read the rules and find the key inputs earlier.** The Capstone FAQ gives 0.999999 as the highest valid input, yet I submitted exactly 1.000000 for F5 in six rounds, and the portal accepted it. Working out sooner which inputs drive each function (F5's x3 and x4, F8's x1, x3 and x7) would also have let me step along them from the start.

## Trade-offs and decisions

Beyond exploration and exploitation, these trade-offs shaped my decisions.

**Trusting a model versus measuring directly.** The Gaussian Process gave a prediction and an uncertainty for every candidate. But it was overconfident: only 49% of its 39 predictions landed within one standard deviation, against about 68% for a well-calibrated model. Where it failed I stepped away from its guidance and measured the function directly with small steps, giving up a model's judgement for slower but more reliable information.

**Flexibility versus reliability.** The more flexible model did not always help. The Random Forest coped with F5's scale and F1's flat landscape but could never predict above the best value it had seen, so it was no use for small improving steps. I kept whichever model had predicted well for each function, not the most sophisticated one.

**Short-term gains versus long-term learning.** Each weekly query could go to a safe refinement or an informative probe, and never both. When a function was climbing I took refinements. Where there was little to lose, or the models had run out of ideas, I spent queries on probes: F1 was explored aggressively through its flat weeks, while F8, on a steady streak, was kept safe. Probes paid off sometimes (F4's step at right angles gained 0.058 on the last query) and not others (F6's repeated step lost 0.014).

**Judgement versus reproducibility.** Choosing between models and settings by hand let me react to each result, but it makes my process harder to reproduce exactly. I limited this by fixing the random seed, saving every week's data unchanged and writing my reasoning into a weekly changelog.

## Learning and application

**The most important lesson.** The most important lesson was to adapt. My plan was to explore first and exploit later, but it only worked because I changed it whenever the evidence showed it was failing. Every improvement followed a mistake I could point to: the repeated queries led to the duplicate check, the overconfident predictions led me to measure functions directly, and F1's flat outputs led to modelling its logarithm and then to a pattern search. Learning from each mistake, and building that learning into the process, mattered more than any single technique.

Documenting every action is something I will apply everywhere: my weekly changelog is the reason I can explain each decision, and the few settings I did not record are the ones I cannot justify. Researching around the problem and trying new methods is what improved my code. And in future ML projects I will outline the key objectives very early, deciding what to measure and record before the first query, not after.

**Applying the lessons.** I will write down objectives and success measures at the start, log each decision with its reason as it happens, and test a model's confidence against real outcomes before trusting it. I will also use BoTorch in future ML projects, building on the familiarity this capstone has given me. In my own field of payments, the same approach could be used to tune fraud-model thresholds, where every live test has a cost.

**What surprised me.** F1 surprised me most. Almost every result was zero, and it took seven weeks before a method gave me a positive gain. That difficulty forced me to experiment with new methods instead of repeating what was failing, and it taught me not to be disheartened by negative outcomes, because they push you to adapt your method and code. What surprised me next was how much changed once I did: F1 eventually rose by eleven orders of magnitude, and in Week 8 every function improved at once. I intend to carry this into future ML projects: try new things, and expect the first attempts to fail.
