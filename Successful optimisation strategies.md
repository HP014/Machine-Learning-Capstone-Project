# Successful optimisation strategies

## Which strategies led to my strongest results

My best results did not come from one clever technique. They came from a sequence of changes, each a response to a specific failure. Here they are in the order I made them, with what each tried to fix, what it achieved and what I learnt, including the ones that did not work.

**Gaussian Process with UCB and EI (Week 1).** The starting point from the course was a Gaussian Process per function, scoring 10,000 random candidates with Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) and Expected Improvement (EI). It found F5's large peak (2,987) in Week 1 and lifted F4 from -4.03 to 0.37 by Week 3. But it also kept proposing points I had already sampled, and followed blindly it gave me four wasted queries. I learnt that an acquisition function is a suggestion to be checked, not an instruction.

**Duplicate check (Week 6).** To fix the repeats, I excluded any candidate within 0.001 of a sampled point. No query has been repeated since. With one query a week, the check saved more than any model change, and I would build it on day one next time.

**Random Forest (Week 6), which failed.** Out of curiosity, I added a Random Forest to see whether a different kind of model could help where the Gaussian Process had no signal, as on F5 and F1. At first it looked promising: its F5 prediction (2,479) landed near my best known point, and all four of its picks fell within about 1.1 standard deviations of their predictions. Yet none of the four set a new best, and I stopped choosing its picks from Week 7. A forest averages outputs it has already seen, so it can never predict above the best value in its data, which is useless for small improving steps. With 35 points in four dimensions it also extrapolated noise on F4 (a prediction of -6.65 when every observed value lay between -1.28 and 0.37). Honest uncertainty was not enough, because the model could not point anywhere useful. The lesson was that a different model is not automatically a better one. It also made me start logging every prediction against its outcome, and that log later showed my Gaussian Process was overconfident.

**F1 log transform (Week 7).** F1's outputs ranged from 1e-245 to 1e-3. Squared error treats everything near zero as identical, so neither model could tell points apart. Fitting the model to log10 of the absolute output made the pattern visible, and F1 improved for the first time (7.7e-16 to 1.9e-6). It discards the sign of the output, so it finds the active region and not the exact peak. This was my most effective change, and it came from understanding the data and not from a fancier model.

**Local search (Week 7).** For F3 and F4, the global search chose far-off points that the models themselves predicted to be poor (F4's Gaussian Process predicted -13 against a best of 0.37). I searched a box of plus or minus 0.04 around each best point instead. F4 recovered from -2.83 to 0.27 and F3 set a new best. The cost is that a local search can refine a peak but never find another one.

**Exploitation switch (Week 8).** Wherever I took a wide jump the results were poor, and wherever I stayed near a good point they improved, so I moved to a box of plus or minus 0.02 around each best point. I also floored the length scale at 0.05 and normalised the outputs, because the fits for F2 and F5 had collapsed into spiky, near white-noise kernels (length scales of 0.0085 and 1e-5). All eight functions set a new best for the first time, and 31 of my 46 new bests came from Week 8 onwards. This was the turning point of the project.

**Direct measurement (Weeks 8 to 13).** My Gaussian Process predictions were within one standard deviation only 49% of the time (39 predictions), so where they failed I measured the function directly. For F1, a Gaussian bump fitted to the positive outputs failed a hold-out test (it predicted 1e-31 for a value that was really 1e-5.7), so I switched to a pattern search, and one step of +0.012 multiplied the output by 12. For F4 and F6 I used line searches and half steps, and F4's line search predicted 0.645 and returned 0.657. Not every refinement worked. A curve fitted through F1's profile predicted +2.2e-4 and returned -2.14e-4. A local quadratic fit for F3, F4 and F6 had hold-out errors as large as the spread of the data, so I dropped it. A Gaussian Process with a separate length scale per input predicted F4 better than the plain one (error 0.30 against 0.48) but was still overconfident. The lesson was that any structure I fit needs a hold-out test before I trust it.

**Narrow peaks on F2 and F3 (Weeks 8 to 13).** These two functions showed me how sharp a peak can be. F2 never beat its starting 0.611 in the first seven weeks, reached 0.667 in Week 8, and then stayed there for five weeks. Its fitted Gaussian Process kept collapsing, and moves of just 0.003 in an input changed the output by 0.04 to 0.12. So I probed one input at a time. Moving x1 down lost 0.063 and moving it up lost 0.037, which bracketed the peak. For x2, a curve through three points on its profile put the peak at 0.908 with a predicted value of about 0.72, and I queried 0.9095. It returned 0.742 on the final query, beating both the prediction and the old best. F3 behaved differently. After its best of -0.0054 in Week 9, every move I tried lost between 0.007 and 0.0098 whatever the distance, which looked like a narrow spike on a flat background of about -0.013 to -0.015. A curve through the x3 values showed that most of the loss came from x3 drifting away from about 0.4545, while a diagonal climb in x1 and x2 had lost very little. A last step lowering x3 by 0.006 lost only 0.0038, less than the earlier moves, so the peak may be broader than I assumed. F3 never beat its Week 9 best, so I count it as a lesson and not a success. With one query a week, a narrow peak in three dimensions needs more probes than I had. Both functions taught me that changing one input at a time tells you which input matters, and that a simple one-dimensional curve can beat a complex model when data are scarce.

**Kappa.** In the small boxes, raising kappa from 0 to 2 moved most picks by under 0.03, so I treated the box size as my exploration setting and left kappa alone.

Together these changes took F5 from 2,987 to 4,246 (up 42%), and F1 rose by eleven orders of magnitude. They also changed how I decided: I moved from exploring first to exploiting once the evidence supported it, treated model predictions as hints, and chose each week's query by asking what the last result had shown.

## What defines a successful strategy

Outcomes matter, and F5's 42% gain is a real result. But outcomes alone mislead. A lucky query teaches nothing, and F3 and F6 stalled even with a good method, which suggests narrow peaks that one query a week was too slow to find. I think a successful strategy needs four qualities.

**Adaptability** matters most. F1 gave almost nothing for seven weeks. Repeating what was failing would have kept it flat. Changing the model, then the transform, then the whole method is what escaped the plateau. **Reasoning and justification** come next. I kept a weekly changelog giving the reason for every action, so I can retrace my steps and a reader can follow the whole story in order. The few settings with no recorded reason are the ones I cannot defend. **Efficiency** matters when queries are scarce, as the duplicate check showed. **Honesty about uncertainty** matters too: comparing predictions with outcomes told me when to stop trusting the model.

## Applying these strategies beyond the capstone

- **Check the data before choosing the model.** Heavy-tailed targets such as transaction amounts or fraud scores often need a log transform, as F1 did.
- **Build safeguards and logs first.** Experiment tracking and duplicate checks stop wasted runs in hyperparameter tuning and in live A/B tests.
- **Test a model's confidence against outcomes.** Logging predicted against actual values shows whether its uncertainty can be trusted.
- **Switch phases on evidence.** Explore while information is cheap and refine locally once a good region is found. In my own field of payments, this could tune fraud-model thresholds, where every live test has a cost.
- **Document every decision with its reason.** In a team, that record is what lets someone else audit, reproduce and improve the work.

## What I noticed in my peers' approaches

Several peers used acquisition functions that differed from mine, sometimes a different one for each function. That taught me to approach each function through a different lens, since all eight behave differently. I had used UCB and EI for every function and only varied the settings. Others used libraries such as BoTorch, and AutoML tools, which I plan to explore in my own ML projects. One peer raised kappa in UCB to an extreme value of 1000 and reached an output of 8,267 on F5, against my 4,246.

I see overlap too. The F1 log transform was common among my peers, which suggests it was the natural answer to that function's scale. We also shared the same core idea, a surrogate model guiding the next query.

The kappa of 1000 is interesting because my own check showed kappa barely changed my picks inside a small box. Over the whole space, a very large kappa makes UCB almost pure exploration, which suits a function with a hidden peak. F5 shows the contrast. I found its first peak (2,987) in Week 1 and then stayed in that region for seven weeks. Once I took small steps it rose in every one of the last six rounds, to 4,246, with two inputs held at the 1.000000 boundary. But I was climbing a local hill. My Gaussian Process quoted a standard deviation of only 3 to 5 there, so its uncertainty was no help in judging whether a better region existed. My peer's extreme exploration reached an output almost twice as high.

## Suggestions and how their reflections broadened my view

To strengthen those strategies, I would suggest pairing an extreme kappa with local refinement once the peak is found, as my Week 8 box search did, and logging the effect of each choice of acquisition function so the best one can be identified for each function. I would also suggest hold-out checks on predictions, because that is how I learnt not to trust my own.

My peers broadened my view of success in three ways. First, documentation is essential: collating information as you go and justifying every action, however small, gives a story that shows what truly contributed most and how to improve. Second, take risks and experiment beyond what feels normal when you have the bandwidth. Third, there is more to success than a high output. Maximising the output is the goal, but especially in a first Bayesian optimisation project, we can also learn from our methods and improve them for the future. Each mistake teaches something, and a worse result is not a failure. Think of exploring a dark room with a flashlight: every query lights up a small patch, and even a dim patch tells you where not to look. Slowly the whole room appears.
